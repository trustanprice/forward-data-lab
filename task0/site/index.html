A field test of naive top-k retrieval

The evidence was already there. Nothing used it.

I tested Asta against Google Scholar on a real research question, found a specific gap in how it weighs evidence, then built a minimal version of its pipeline — small enough to fully inspect — and ran it live to see whether the gap actually shows up.

01 · testing asta vs. scholar

I ran the same natural-language research question through both Asta and Google Scholar. Scholar returned nothing — "did not match any articles" — until I manually rewrote it as a keyword string. Asta did that rewriting itself, silently, before searching a corpus of 12M+ papers.

02 · under the hood

Asta's own step log reveals the real pipeline: reformulate the query, retrieve broadly, rerank, then cap the pool to roughly 50 papers before writing a single sentence. Every citation I checked was real — not hallucinated. But nothing after the cap step verifies the shortlist is actually right.

03 · the gap that motivated this

Asta surfaces citation counts if you go looking — a 3-citation 2026 paper and a 16,950-citation 2020 paper both sit in its source list. In the generated narrative, they're treated as equally strong evidence. The data to tell them apart was there the whole time, unused.

So I built a minimal version of that same retrieve → rerank → cap → generate pipeline — and ran it live against Claude Opus 5 to see whether the same gap shows up.

Naive pipeline — Fix 1 disabled

The honest surprise

With the relevance gate turned off, the pipeline still called the model on both queries. One was answerable. One wasn't. Only one of those two outcomes turned out to be guaranteed.

Retrieval itself is unambiguously fragile — these are noise-level similarity scores.

But this recovery came from one model's prompt-following behavior in one test. Not deterministic. Not free — a full paid API call happened either way. Not inspectable in advance. And not what Asta itself was observed doing.

That's the case for Fix 1.

Fix 1 — relevance threshold

Same outcome. Different reason to trust it.

Enforcing the threshold by default doesn't change what happens for the off-topic query — it changes why it happens.

Fix 2 — citation-weighted reranking

Reranking changes prominence, not survival

Blending normalized similarity with log-scaled citation count, within the retrieved pool, moves the field's most established paper from the middle of the pack toward the top.

What this doesn't fix

  1. At top_k=5 instead of 8, the seminal paper — rank 6 by pure similarity — is excluded from the candidate pool before reranking ever runs. Verified directly: it can't rescue what similarity already cut.
  2. Its effect on the generated narrative was smaller than its effect on ranking in this test — likely because citation counts are already visible as plain text in the model's context, regardless of numbering order.

Command

python -m src.generation.generate \ "..." --rerank

Blend: log-scaled citation count + normalized similarity, weighted 50/50, within the retrieved pool.

Top-k still decides who's in the room.

Where this leaves things

Retrieval is fragile. Recovery shouldn't be optional.

Retrieval is unambiguously fragile — noise-level similarity scores on an off-topic query, visible directly in the numbers. Whether a generated report reflects that depended entirely on one model's judgment call, until Fix 1 turned it into a mechanical guarantee. Fix 2 provably reorders the ranking and protects against exclusion at the top-k boundary — but its effect on this particular model's narrative was more modest than expected. That's a real finding, not a shortcoming to hide.

→

Proposed next layer: faithfulness verification

After generation, mechanically check that each inline citation's claim is actually supported by that paper's abstract — the same category of fix as Fix 1: replacing "hope the model got it right" with something inspectable and guaranteed, applied one step later in the pipeline.
